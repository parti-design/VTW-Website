You found the place. Maybe it is a center-hall colonial outside Red Hook, a tired farmhouse on a few acres in Columbia County, or a brick house in Hudson that has been chopped into apartments and you want to put back together. The listing photos are warm, the asking price feels almost reasonable, and your inspection contingency clock is already running. You like it. The question keeping you up is the one a home inspection alone will not fully answer: what am I actually taking on here?

That is the question I get hired to answer. A standard home inspection is genuinely useful, and I am not telling you to skip it. But an inspector documents condition on the day they visit. They are not your advocate at the closing table, and they usually are not pricing out the first two years of ownership. When I walk an old house with a buyer, I am trying to separate the cosmetic from the structural, the annoying from the expensive, and the "live with it" from the "deal with it before you move a wall."

## What should I check first, structure or systems?

Structure and water, always, before anything else. Everything downstream depends on those two.

In this region that means starting underneath, at the foundation. A lot of the older houses I look at in Dutchess and Columbia sit on dry-laid stone or rubble foundations, sometimes with a later poured or block addition tacked on. Dry-laid stone is not automatically a problem; plenty of these foundations have held for a century and a half. But I want to see whether stones have shifted, whether there is fresh mortar smeared over old cracks to hide movement, and whether the sill plate resting on that stone is sound or quietly turning to powder where it meets damp masonry. I look at framing the same way: where additions met the original house, where someone removed a wall and may or may not have carried the load properly, and whether floors and door frames tell me the building is still moving.

Then water, because in older buildings water is almost always the most expensive story. I want roof drainage, gutters, and especially site grading that pushes water away from the house instead of toward it. I want the moisture history of the basement or crawlspace, not just whether it happens to be dry the afternoon we visit. The pattern I distrust is repeated patching: a sump here, a smear of hydraulic cement there, a dehumidifier running year-round. That usually means someone managed the symptom for years and never solved the cause, and you would be buying the unsolved cause.

## How do I know if the systems are actually adequate?

Here is the trap: a system can work today and still be a problem. The furnace fires, the lights turn on, water comes out of the tap, the inspector checks the boxes. What I am assessing is capacity and remaining life against how *you* intend to live there.

A few things I see constantly in Hudson Valley houses:

- **Electrical.** Knob-and-tube wiring still hiding in walls and attics, undersized panels, and service that was fine for a 1950s household but will not carry a heat pump, an EV charger, and a modern kitchen. It may pass as functional and still need a real upgrade.
- **Plumbing.** Galvanized supply lines near the end of their life, and a lot of properties on private septic and well. Out here that means you want a septic inspection and ideally a perc test on file, plus water quality and quantity testing. A failed or undersized septic system is a serious number, and it does not show up in a quick look.
- **Heating and the envelope.** Old steam or oil systems that run but cost a fortune, paired with little to no insulation behind that horsehair plaster. Drafts, cold floors, ice dams. None of it means the house is bad; it means you should know whether you are buying comfort or a comfort project, because that is where a lot of first-year money quietly goes.

## What about permits and all the work the last owner did?

This is the area buyers underestimate the most. Older houses are rarely one clean build. They are original construction plus a 1970s addition plus a finished basement plus a deck someone put on over a weekend. Some of that was permitted and inspected. Some of it was not.

Before you close, it is worth confirming with the local building department what was actually permitted, what open permits might exist, and whether legacy work could trigger upgrade requirements once you touch it. Whether something is code-compliant is the building department's call, not mine and not your inspector's, but I can flag the spots most likely to become a conversation with them. This matters even more in a local historic district, where exterior changes can need extra review. And understand that your renovation plans can change what compliance looks like: a kitchen refresh is one world; opening up the back of the house is another.

## So what do I actually do with all this before closing?

You build a plan and a number. The point of looking this hard is not to talk you out of the house. It is to walk in knowing roughly what the first two years cost and in what order the work should happen.

The sequence I almost always recommend is the same: stabilization first (anything structural, anything letting water in), then system reliability (electrical, septic, heat you can trust), then the envelope (insulation, air-sealing, comfort), and cosmetics last. That order protects both the building and your budget, and it is the opposite of how most people are tempted to spend, which is to make it pretty first. If you want a sense of where renovation dollars really go, [Hudson Valley renovation costs]({% post_url 2025-06-07-hudson-valley-renovation-costs %}) lays it out.

One practical note: good tradespeople up here book out months in advance, especially for foundation, septic, and timber work. If your plan depends on getting a mason or a well contractor in quickly after closing, find out now whether that is realistic.
