You found an architect you trust. The early sketches are beautiful. The plan opens up that dark center-hall farmhouse outside Rhinebeck exactly the way you pictured, and you are excited. Then the first real cost estimate comes back, and it is not close. Now you are staring at a design you love and a number you cannot reach, wondering which one has to give.

I see this often, and it is rarely anyone's fault. A good architect is doing exactly what you hired them to do: solving for light, flow, proportion, and how you want to live in the house. What I do is sit on your side of the table and ask a different set of questions while those drawings are still on paper, when changes cost almost nothing. How does this actually get built? What will it really cost? And where can we hold the line on quality without spending money where it does not show?

## Why does a design come in over budget?

A design rarely blows the budget on one dramatic choice. It happens through dozens of reasonable decisions that each add a little, none of them obviously wrong on their own. A roofline with several intersecting planes. A wall of windows on the north side. A foundation stepping down a sloped Columbia County lot toward a wet corner. A kitchen that quietly assumes the old timber-frame structure will cooperate when you remove a bearing wall.

On older Hudson Valley houses, the building itself adds cost the drawings cannot fully predict. Hand-hewn framing is rarely square or level. Foundations are dry-laid stone that does not love new point loads. There is ledge close to the surface in much of Ulster and Dutchess, and an existing septic field that may push an addition somewhere less convenient than the design assumes. None of this means the design is bad. It means the design needs a reality check from someone who has built in these conditions, early, before the drawings are final and a contractor is pricing them.

My role here is worth stating plainly: I do not design your building, and I do not produce or seal drawings. Your architect and a licensed engineer do that. I review what they produce for constructability and cost, and I translate between the design and the field so the three of us are solving the same problem.

## What does a constructability and cost review actually look like?

When I go through a set of drawings, I am reading them the way the contractor and the framing crew eventually will. I am looking for the gap between what is drawn and what is buildable on your particular lot, with the trades and materials actually available up here.

A few of the things I flag:

- **Details that are expensive to build but easy to draw.** A flush transition, a hidden gutter, a long unsupported span, a complicated roof valley. Each is achievable. Together they can quietly add a large share of the cost, and sometimes a simpler detail reads almost identically once it is built.
- **Assumptions about existing conditions.** When a plan depends on what is behind the plaster or under the floor in a two-hundred-year-old house, I want that verified before it is locked in, not discovered during demolition. Surprises found on paper are cheap. Surprises found on site become [change orders]({% post_url 2025-12-03-how-to-avoid-change-orders %}).
- **Coordination gaps between disciplines.** Where the structural approach, the mechanical runs, and the architecture all meet is where money and weeks disappear. If the engineer's beam wants to live exactly where the design wants a clean ceiling, better to surface that now and let the licensed professionals resolve it than to have a framer call you from the site.
- **Sequence and access.** A narrow lane in Millbrook, a steep drive in Woodstock, winter conditions that shut down certain work for months. How a thing gets built, and when, is part of what it costs.

I bring these observations back to your architect as questions and options, not instructions. The design intent stays theirs. I am making sure it survives contact with a budget and a job site.

## How do you value engineer without cheapening the house?

"Value engineering" has earned a bad name because it is too often code for stripping out everything good until the number works. Done right, it is the opposite. It is deciding, deliberately, where every dollar earns its keep.

The way I think about it: protect the things you feel and the things you cannot easily change later, and find savings in the things you never notice. Spend on the windows you look through every morning, the floors underfoot, the proportions of the rooms you live in, the structural and weatherproofing work that keeps a Hudson Valley house standing through hard winters. Look for savings in the framing geometry, the mechanical routing, the finish you specified out of habit rather than love, the detail that costs three times as much for a difference no guest will ever register.

Often the biggest savings come not from cheaper materials but from a simpler way to build the same idea. A roof with one less plane. A structural approach the engineer signs off on that needs less steel. A layout shift that keeps the new bathroom stacked over existing plumbing. Those changes hold the design's quality and take real money out, and they are exactly the conversations I am built to have with your architect and engineer.

## How does an owner's rep work alongside my architect and engineer?

I am not a replacement for your architect, and I am not a referee. Think of me as the builder's judgment, brought to your side of the table before anyone breaks ground. The architect shapes the vision. The engineer makes it stand up and seals the drawings. I make sure the path from those drawings to a finished house is realistic, priced honestly, and free of the avoidable surprises that turn a good project into a stressful one.
