A client in Rhinebeck called me last winter with a question I hear all the time. She'd gotten three different pitches: one company wanted to sell her a new heat pump system, another wanted to blow insulation into the attic, and a solar installer wanted to talk panels. Everyone had a brochure, everyone had a rebate to mention, and she had no idea which one to do first or whether any of it was worth the money in a drafty 1890s farmhouse. That's the right question, and the order matters more than most people realize.

I'm not an energy auditor and I'm not selling equipment. I'm the builder's judgment on your side of the table, and over the years I've watched a lot of homeowners spend real money on the wrong thing in the wrong order. So here's how I think about green upgrades in our climate, plainly.

## What should I do first, the envelope or the equipment?

The envelope. Almost always, the envelope first.

Your "envelope" is the shell of the house: air sealing, insulation, windows, the basement and attic. The mechanical equipment, the furnace or heat pump, is just the engine that fights whatever the shell lets through. If you buy a beautiful new heating system before you've tightened the shell, you're sizing and paying for equipment to overcome leaks you could have closed for less money.

In an old Hudson Valley house, the leaks are usually the headline. A center-hall Colonial outside Millbrook or a converted farmhouse in Columbia County often has an uninsulated attic floor, a fieldstone foundation with a cold dirt-floor cellar, balloon framing that lets air run straight from the basement to the roof, and original windows with gaps you can feel on a January night. Fix the air leaks and the attic insulation first, and the house gets more comfortable immediately, the rooms stop feeling drafty, and whatever heating system you eventually choose can be smaller and run more efficiently.

The unglamorous order I usually suggest: air sealing, then attic and basement/crawlspace insulation, then look hard at the heating system, and only then think about windows and the bigger-ticket items. Air sealing is often the cheapest dollar-for-comfort move you can make, and it's the one nobody puts on a brochure.

## Do cold-climate heat pumps actually work in Hudson Valley winters?

Yes, with honest caveats. The cold-climate air-source heat pumps on the market now are a real improvement over the units people remember from a decade or two ago. They're designed to keep producing usable heat well into single-digit temperatures, which covers most of what Dutchess, Ulster, and Columbia counties throw at us most of the year.

The honest part: efficiency does drop as it gets colder, and we do get those stretches below zero. That's why I tell people to plan for capacity and a backup strategy rather than assume the heat pump alone will carry the worst week of February. Some owners keep an existing furnace or boiler as backup. Some add electric resistance backup. Either way, the sizing and the backup plan should be worked out by a qualified mechanical contractor who actually does a load calculation on your house, not a rule-of-thumb guess off the old furnace's nameplate.

And this is exactly why the envelope comes first. A leaky house makes the heat pump look bad in a cold snap, because it's trying to heat a building that won't hold heat. Tighten the shell and the same equipment performs better on the coldest days. The two decisions are connected; treat them as one project, not two.

One more practical note for our area: a lot of older homes here run on propane or oil, and some have no ductwork at all. Ductless mini-splits can be a sensible fit for those houses, but how many heads you need, where the lines run, and how the work lands on old framing and plaster are real constructability questions worth thinking through before anyone drills a hole in a 200-year-old wall. Where the heads go and how they sit in a historic interior is worth settling with your installer, and with your design professional if you're using one, before the install, not after. My job there is to keep the plan buildable and coordinated, and to make sure the comfort you're paying for actually shows up.

## What's genuinely worth it, and what usually isn't?

Worth it, in roughly this order:

- **Air sealing.** Cheap, high return, improves comfort right away.
- **Attic insulation.** Heat goes up and out; in an under-insulated old house this pays back fast.
- **Basement or crawlspace work.** Cold, damp cellars under old Hudson Valley homes pull heat out of the floors above and can drive moisture problems if ignored.
- **A right-sized cold-climate heat pump,** once the shell is improved and the load is actually calculated.

Usually oversold:

- **Full window replacement as an energy move.** New windows are expensive, and on an old house the payback purely on energy is slow. Storm windows and weatherstripping often get you most of the comfort for a fraction of the cost, and they keep the character. Replace windows when they're truly failing or you want them, not because someone promised dramatic savings.
- **Big equipment before the envelope.** Covered above, but it's the most common expensive mistake I see.

The other quiet truth: old houses need to breathe and stay dry. Sealing a damp fieldstone cellar tight without addressing moisture can trade an energy problem for a rot or mold problem. Building science in old buildings is about managing air and water together, which is one more reason to sequence the work and not just chase the biggest rebate.

## What about NYSERDA and the Clean Heat rebates?

They exist, they can be meaningful, and they change. New York runs incentive programs through NYSERDA, and there's the NYS Clean Heat program for heat pumps, alongside a mix of utility rebates and federal tax provisions that come and go. I'm deliberately not quoting dollar amounts or percentages here, because the numbers, the eligibility rules, and the contractor requirements get revised, and last year's figure can lead you astray.

What I'd tell you to do: decide what's right for the house first, on the merits, then check the current programs to see what's available for that work. Many incentives require you to use a participating contractor or follow a specific process, so confirm the current rules before you sign anything. Verify the present details directly with NYSERDA, NYS Clean Heat, and your utility, and treat any rebate as a bonus on a good decision, not the reason for it.
