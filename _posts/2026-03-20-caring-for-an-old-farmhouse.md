There's a particular sound an old house makes when it's been raining for three days straight. You're lying awake in a farmhouse outside Rhinebeck or up a dirt road in Columbia County, and you hear it: a slow tick somewhere in the wall, or a smell of damp coming up from the cellar that wasn't there last week. Most of the time it's nothing. But if you've ever wondered whether you're staying ahead of your old house or quietly falling behind it, you're asking the right question.

I've spent a lot of years walking through old barns and farmhouses across the Hudson Valley, and I can tell you the single biggest truth about keeping one: the expensive problems are almost always slow problems. A rotted sill, a failed chimney, a basement that finally gives up after twenty wet springs. None of them happen overnight. All of them start small. The good news is that the small stuff is cheap, and most of it you can handle yourself with a ladder, a few hours, and a habit of paying attention.

## Why is water the thing I should worry about most?

Because water is what takes these houses down. Not age, not the harsh winters on their own. Water. Everything else on your maintenance list is really just water management wearing a different hat.

Start where the water lands and follow it down. Your gutters should be clear at least twice a year, spring and fall, and more often if you've got big maples leaning over the roof the way half the houses in Woodstock do. A clogged gutter doesn't just overflow. It dumps water straight down against your foundation, exactly where you don't want it, and on an old fieldstone or rubble foundation that water finds every gap. Make sure your downspouts actually carry water away from the house, not just to the base of the wall. A four-foot extension on a downspout is the cheapest foundation insurance you will ever buy.

Then look at your grading. The ground should slope away from the house on every side. Over decades, soil settles, gardens get built up, and a lot of old farmhouses end up sitting in a shallow bowl that funnels rain right back at them. Walk the perimeter after a heavy rain and see where the water wants to go. If it pools against the wall, you've found a problem worth fixing before it finds your basement.

Down in the cellar, a little seasonal dampness is normal in these old stone foundations. Standing water, a high-water mark on the wall, or efflorescence (that white powdery crust on masonry) tells you water is getting in regularly. Catch it early and it's a grading and gutter fix. Ignore it and it becomes a structural one.

## What about the roof, chimney, and masonry?

You don't need to climb up there in January. But twice a year, and after any big windstorm, take a good look from the ground with binoculars. You're looking for lifted or missing shingles, and especially for the condition of the flashing, the metal that seals the joints around chimneys, valleys, and where a roof meets a wall. Flashing fails long before the shingles do, and a failed flashing detail is behind a surprising number of the mystery leaks I get called about.

Chimneys take a beating here. Our freeze-thaw cycles are brutal on masonry: water gets into a tiny crack in the mortar, freezes, expands, and opens the crack a little wider every winter. Look for crumbling mortar joints, a cracked crown at the top, or a chimney that's started to lean. Repointing, when a mason carefully replaces failing mortar, is routine maintenance on an old house, not a disaster. The disaster is the chimney you let go until it has to be rebuilt. If your house has an old brick or stone foundation, give that the same eye. Soft, sandy, falling-out mortar is telling you something.

One craft note worth respecting: old masonry was built with soft lime mortar, and patching it with modern hard portland cement can actually trap moisture and accelerate the damage. This is a place to get the right advice rather than reach for whatever's at the hardware store.

## How do I keep the wood and paint from going?

On a wood house, paint isn't decoration. It's the raincoat. The moment paint fails on a window sill, a clapboard end, or a porch post, bare wood starts drinking, and wet wood in the Hudson Valley means rot, and eventually carpenter ants and other pests that move into the soft wood you've kindly provided.

Walk your house once a year and look at the vulnerable spots: south and west walls that take the weather, sills, the bottoms of trim boards, anywhere two pieces of wood meet. Scrape, prime, and spot-paint the small failures while they're small. Keep shrubs and firewood pulled back off the walls so wood can dry and so you're not building a bridge for ants and mice. Trim back branches touching the roof. It's humble work, and it quietly prevents the kind of carpentry bills that make people wince.

## What's the rhythm that keeps it all from piling up?

Think of it in seasons. In fall, clean gutters, look at the roof before the snow, and make sure water drains away before the ground freezes. In spring, clean gutters again, walk the foundation and cellar for winter damage, and check masonry and flashing. In summer, tackle the paint and woodwork while it's dry. A weekend or two a year, spread out, is genuinely most of it.
