You have three proposals spread across the kitchen table. One is noticeably cheaper than the other two, and your eye keeps drifting back to it. The other two contractors seemed sharper when they walked the house, but that lower number is hard to ignore. So you sit there asking the question almost everyone asks me at this stage: how do I know which of these is actually the right one?

Price matters. I would never tell you it doesn't. But price alone is a poor way to decide, and in the Hudson Valley it can be an actively dangerous one. The right contractor for a tidy kitchen update in Rhinebeck may be exactly the wrong contractor for an 1840s farmhouse with a dry-laid stone foundation and horsehair plaster on the walls. Choosing well starts long before you compare numbers.

## Why does choosing a contractor work differently up here?

Most of what I do is renovation and restoration of old houses and barns, and that changes the math. A lot of the building stock in Dutchess, Columbia, and Ulster counties is genuinely old: timber-frame barns, balloon-framed farmhouses, knob-and-tube wiring still live in a corner of the attic, lime mortar in the foundation that will spall and crumble if someone repoints it with modern Portland cement.

A contractor who is excellent at straightforward work may have never touched any of that. That is not a knock on them; it is a question of fit. Pointing a stone foundation, splicing into a hand-hewn sill, matching old trim profiles, plastering over lath instead of hanging drywall over it, working around an old septic field and ledge rock you cannot just dig through — these are specific skills. The person who is best at them is often not the person with the lowest overhead or the flashiest truck.

So before you weigh price, get honest about what your project actually is. A near-new house in a Kingston subdivision and a tired barn outside Millbrook are not the same job, and they do not call for the same crew. If you are still at the buying stage, my [old-house buying checklist]({% post_url 2025-08-29-buying-old-house-hudson-valley-checklist %}) will help you understand what you are taking on before you ever line up bids.

## Why do bids have to be comparable before you read them?

Here is the part owners skip most often. Good selection starts before bids come in. If the scope is vague, the numbers are not truly comparable, no matter how neatly they are laid out.

One contractor includes the new electrical panel, another assumes you will handle it. One carries a real allowance for finishes, another lowballs it so the bottom line looks better. What reads as a bargain on Tuesday becomes expensive in month three, once all the missing work reappears as change orders. The cheapest bid is frequently just the least complete one.

When you do read proposals, look at the whole picture, not the total. What is included, what is explicitly excluded, how allowances are structured, whether the schedule assumptions are realistic, and how clearly the contractor writes things down. The pre-construction process tells you how the construction process will feel. A contractor who is vague and slow to put things in writing now will be vague and slow when there is money on the line. I wrote more about where these gaps come from in [why renovations go over budget]({% post_url 2026-02-27-why-renovations-go-over-budget %}) and how to head them off in [how to avoid change orders]({% post_url 2025-12-03-how-to-avoid-change-orders %}).

## What should you actually ask their references?

Reference checks are most useful when they are specific. "Were you happy?" gets you a polite yes and tells you nothing. Ask the questions that reveal how the team behaves under pressure:

- How were costs tracked, and were you surprised by anything at the end?
- How were changes handled when they came up, on paper or with a handshake?
- Were timelines updated honestly, or did "next week" keep sliding?
- Would you hire the same team again, and is there anything you would do differently?

Just as important: check references from projects like yours. A glowing review of a new garage in Hudson does not tell you whether the contractor can stabilize a leaning post-and-beam barn or repoint a stone cellar. Ask to see, and ideally to visit, an old house they restored. The work either holds up in person or it doesn't.

## How early can you book the right crew?

The good ones are busy, and that is its own piece of information. Skilled restoration trades in this region get booked months out, sometimes the better part of a year for specialized work like timber framing, slate roofing, or real plaster. If someone qualified for a complex old house can start next week, ask yourself why.

Plan around that reality instead of fighting it. Start conversations early, accept that a realistic start date may be a season away, and treat a contractor who is honest about their backlog as a good sign, not a problem. The crew who tells you the truth about their calendar will usually tell you the truth about your budget too.

## What are the warning signs?

Watch for the patterns that rarely improve once work begins: a number that is far below the others with no clear reasoning, big gaps in scope, communication that is already inconsistent during courtship, and resistance to clear documentation. None of those get better after the deposit clears.

The right choice is almost never simply the lowest bid on the table. It is the contractor with a transparent process, a realistic plan, and the right experience for your exact scope.
