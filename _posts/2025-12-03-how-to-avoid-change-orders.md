A couple bought a farmhouse outside Rhinebeck and called me three weeks into their renovation. The contract had come in close to what they expected, they liked the builder, and they had signed. Now there was a stack of change orders on the kitchen table and a number on the bottom that was climbing past anything they had budgeted. None of it felt like anyone's fault, exactly. The framing under the bathroom was rotted. The "as needed" electrical line had turned into a whole-house rewire. The kitchen cabinet allowance bought maybe half the cabinets. They asked me the question I hear most often: how does a project go from on-budget to over-budget without anyone deciding it should?

The honest answer is that most of it was decided before the first wall came down. Change orders are a normal part of construction, and they are not going away. But the expensive, conflict-ridden ones are usually preventable, and the place you prevent them is in the weeks before you sign.

## Why do change orders happen in the first place?

Strip away the drama and almost every change order traces back to one of four things.

The first is **vague scope**. If the drawings and specifications do not say exactly what is being built and with what, the builder has to guess. Guesses get priced low to win the job, and the gap surfaces later as a change order. The second is **thin allowances** — those placeholder numbers for things you have not picked yet, like tile, fixtures, or cabinets. When the allowance is set unrealistically low to keep the contract total attractive, the difference is not savings. It is a change order waiting to happen the moment you choose anything real. The third is **no contingency**. A renovation with no money set aside for the unexpected treats every surprise as a crisis. And the fourth, the one that defines old Hudson Valley houses, is **hidden conditions**: things nobody could see until demolition opened the wall.

I dig into the budget side of this more in [why renovations go over budget]({% post_url 2026-02-27-why-renovations-go-over-budget %}), but the short version is that drift is rarely one big bad decision. It is a hundred small ones nobody wrote down.

## Why do old houses generate so many change orders?

This is the part specific to where we build. A new house in a subdivision has few secrets. An 1840s farmhouse in Columbia County, a Greek Revival in Hudson, a center-hall colonial outside Millbrook — these are full of them, and you cannot price what you cannot see.

I have opened walls and found knob-and-tube wiring spliced into modern circuits, horsehair plaster hiding water damage two generations old, and sill beams turned to powder where a gutter failed decades ago. Dry-laid stone foundations in Ulster County look fine until you load them differently. Old framing was rarely square or consistent, so the new bathroom that looked simple on paper needs the floor leveled before anything else can happen. Outside the walls, the ground has its own surprises: ledge where you wanted to trench, a septic field that fails perc and forces a redesign, a well line nobody mapped.

None of that is the builder being difficult. It is the building telling you something it could not tell you until it was open. The mistake is pretending it will not happen. The fix is not wishful budgeting — it is **investigating early**. Before you sign, I push for targeted exploration: pull a section of plaster, open a soffit, get under the house, test the things that drive the biggest numbers. A few hundred dollars of demolition during planning can move a foundation or framing problem from a panic-priced change order into a known line item. The same conditions you should be checking before you buy an old house should also be informing the contract you are about to sign.

## What should I do before I sign the contract?

This is where the leverage is. Once the contract is signed and the crew is on site, your options narrow and the prices go up. Before you sign, you still have all of it. A few things I insist on:

- **Get the scope as complete as you can.** Your architect's drawings and the builder's specifications should describe the same building. When they disagree, that gap becomes a change order. Coordinating them is exactly the kind of constructability and budget reality-check I work through with your design team — more on that in [design that is buildable]({% post_url 2026-04-03-design-that-is-buildable-working-with-your-architect %}).
- **Pressure-test the allowances.** For every allowance, ask what it actually buys. If the cabinet allowance would not cover the kitchen you have been describing, fix it on paper now, not in month two.
- **Build in a real contingency.** On an old house, a contingency is not padding — it is the price of not knowing. Set the money aside, name it, and decide in advance who can spend it and on what.
- **Read the change-order clause out loud.** How are changes priced — fixed markup, time and materials, a published rate? Who can authorize one? Does work stop until you approve in writing? A contract that lets work proceed on a verbal "go ahead" is a contract that will surprise you.

The cost questions behind all of this are worth understanding on their own; I lay out the moving parts in [Hudson Valley renovation costs]({% post_url 2025-06-07-hudson-valley-renovation-costs %}).

## How do I keep changes under control once work starts?

Even with good planning, some changes are real. The goal is not zero change orders — it is no surprise change orders. That comes down to process discipline. Every proposed change should be written down, priced transparently, tied to its schedule impact, and approved in writing before work proceeds whenever the situation allows. When a hidden condition does turn up, you want a fast decision path: someone who can look at the framing, understand what it means for cost and schedule, tell the difference between a legitimate unforeseen condition and a coordination error somebody is trying to pass to you, and help you decide quickly. Delay is its own cost in this region, where good tradespeople are booked months out and a stalled job loses its place in the queue.
