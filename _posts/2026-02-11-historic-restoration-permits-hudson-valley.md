## Who actually approves historic restoration work?

There is no single "historic permit." For most projects you are dealing with up to three separate layers, and they do not all apply to every property.

The first, and the one that applies to essentially every project, is your **local building department and code official**. This is your town, village, or city office: Rhinebeck, Hudson, Kingston, Millbrook, wherever the property sits. They issue the building permit and they are the ones who determine code compliance. Not your architect, not me, not the State. The code official signs off, and that is the legal gate you have to pass to do most work and to get a certificate of occupancy at the end.

The second layer is a **local historic preservation or landmarks commission**, where one exists. Many Hudson Valley municipalities have them, and they tend to govern designated local landmarks or properties inside a local historic district. If yours does, you may need a **certificate of appropriateness** before the building department will move forward. That review is about how the work affects the building's historic character: windows, siding, roofing, the shape and rhythm of the facade. Whether your property is actually covered, and what triggers a review, varies enormously town to town, so confirm it directly with your municipality early.

The third layer is the **State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO)**, which lives inside the New York State Office of Parks, Recreation and Historic Preservation. SHPO generally enters the picture when your property is listed on the State or National Register, or when you intend to use historic rehabilitation tax credits, where they review the work against preservation standards. If none of that applies, SHPO usually is not part of your project at all. If it does, their review runs in parallel with the local process and on its own timeline.

## Does my project need a certificate of appropriateness?

This is the question I get most, and the honest answer is: it depends on where the property is and how it is designated. A privately owned, undesignated old house out on a county road may answer only to the building department. The same-age house inside a local historic district in Hudson might need commission review for something as ordinary as replacing windows or changing a roof color.

Designation matters more than age. A building can be a hundred and fifty years old and carry no formal protection, while a younger one inside a district is tightly governed. Before you assume either way, call the municipal clerk or building department and ask three things: is the property a local landmark or in a local district, is it on the State or National Register, and what work requires review. Get the answer in writing if you can. I have seen owners spend on detailed plans for a scope that, once the real requirements surfaced, had to be reworked.

## What usually causes the delays?

This is where I can save you the most grief, because the delays are predictable.

The biggest one is **submitting before the scope is actually resolved**. When an application shows up with vague repair intent, undefined structural work, or materials that clash with historic-character expectations, reviewers do the only thing they can: send it back with questions. Each round trip can add weeks, and those weeks ripple into your scheduling. Good local tradespeople, the ones who know lime mortar and timber framing, are booked months out. Miss your window because of a revision cycle and you may wait a season for the right crew.

The second is **incomplete submissions**. Reviewers move faster when intent is explicit: thorough existing-condition documentation, a clearly drawn proposed scope, coherent detail intent, and a sequence that separates urgent stabilization from the broader restoration. If a leaking roof or a failing sill needs immediate attention, that often can be framed as stabilization and handled ahead of the larger package. Muddle the two together and everything waits on the slowest piece.

The third is the one no amount of paperwork prevents: **surprises inside old buildings**. You open a wall and find knob-and-tube wiring, horsehair plaster hiding rot, a dry-laid stone foundation that has quietly shifted, or a barn sill that is more soft than wood. These discoveries change the scope, which can mean going back to the same reviewers you already cleared. I build contingency into both the budget and the calendar precisely because these old buildings always have a story they have not told you yet. This is the same dynamic behind why [renovations go over budget]({% post_url 2026-02-27-why-renovations-go-over-budget %}), and it is worth understanding before you start.

## How should I plan around all this?

Treat approvals as a real phase of the project, not a formality you rush at the end. That means resolving scope before submission, documenting existing conditions honestly, and sequencing the work so stabilization is not held hostage to the full restoration package.

It also means planning your budget and timeline around iteration. Historic reviews often require a round or two of back-and-forth, and that has both cost and schedule consequences. Projects that acknowledge this up front are simply calmer.
