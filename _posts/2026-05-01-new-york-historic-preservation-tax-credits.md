You bought the 1840s farmhouse outside Rhinebeck, or the brick row house in Hudson, and now you are staring down a restoration budget that keeps climbing. The slate roof, the failing plaster, the windows everyone tells you to rip out and shouldn't. Somewhere along the way a neighbor or your accountant mentioned "historic tax credits," and you wondered: is that a real thing, or just something people say?

It is real. I am not a tax professional, and nothing here is tax advice. But I have walked enough owners through historic projects in Dutchess, Columbia, and Ulster counties to know that these programs are worth understanding early, because they can change how you scope and sequence the work. Let me give you the lay of the land.

## What historic tax credits actually do

There are two programs that come up most often for old houses in our region. One is the **New York State Historic Homeownership Rehabilitation Tax Credit**, aimed specifically at owner-occupied homes. The other is the **federal historic rehabilitation tax credit**, which is generally geared toward income-producing properties — a building you rent out or run a business from — and generally is not available for the house you live in. If your situation is mixed (say, a barn you intend to rent, or a property with a commercial use), that is exactly the kind of thing to ask a tax professional about rather than assume.

The basic idea behind both is the same. If you take a qualifying historic building and do qualifying rehabilitation work, a portion of what you spend can come back to you as a credit against taxes you owe. A credit is more valuable than a deduction — it reduces your tax bill more directly. That is the headline. Everything else is conditions.

I am deliberately not quoting you a percentage or a dollar cap, because those numbers change, and the rules around income limits, minimum spending thresholds, and how unused credit carries forward get adjusted over time. Anyone who tells you a precise rate off the top of their head is quoting a figure that may already be stale. Verify the current terms with the New York State Office of Parks, Recreation and Historic Preservation, whose State Historic Preservation Office administers the program, and with a tax professional before you count on anything.

## Could my house even qualify?

This is the first real gate, and it surprises people. The credits are not for any old house — they are for *historic* houses in a specific, documented sense.

Generally, that means the property is either individually listed on the State or National Register of Historic Places, or it sits within a listed historic district and contributes to that district's character. A lot of Hudson Valley homeowners are already in this position without knowing it. Parts of Hudson, Kingston, Rhinebeck, and the villages around Millbrook and Woodstock have districts or eligible properties. Your house being old, or even genuinely historic in spirit, is not the same as being designated. The paperwork has to exist.

The state homeownership program has also historically tied eligibility to where the property sits — often targeting certain census tracts or areas that meet specific economic criteria. That is exactly the kind of detail that gets revised, so confirm whether your specific address qualifies rather than assuming. If you are still in the buying stage, this is worth checking before you close; it can factor into how you think about the whole purchase, alongside the rest of your [old-house due diligence]({% post_url 2025-08-29-buying-old-house-hudson-valley-checklist %}).

## What kind of work counts?

Not everything you spend money on counts as "qualified rehabilitation." Broadly, these programs reward work that repairs and preserves the historic building — structural repairs, roofing, masonry, window restoration, mechanical systems, plaster, the things that keep the building standing and authentic. They generally do not reward new additions, landscaping, new outbuildings, or pure cosmetic upgrades that have nothing to do with the historic fabric.

There is also a preservation-standards dimension. The work usually has to be done in a way that respects the building's historic character — which is why the state office reviews and approves the scope. This is where I see the most friction. An owner wants to swap the old wavy-glass windows for vinyl, and the program wants those windows repaired or replaced in kind. The credit is partly an incentive to do the *right* restoration rather than the cheapest one.

That standards layer is also why these projects need more coordination up front, and why permits and reviews can run longer than a standard renovation. I wrote separately about [historic permits and preservation reviews]({% post_url 2026-02-11-historic-restoration-permits-hudson-valley %}) — the tax-credit review and the local approval process often run on parallel tracks, and it helps to plan them together.

## Where I fit, and where I don't

I want to be clear about my role, because tax credits attract a lot of vague promises. I am a building specialist and owner's representative. I do not prepare your tax filings, I do not seal drawings, and I am not the one who certifies your application with the state. You will want a tax professional for the numbers and, depending on the project, your licensed architect or engineer for the drawings and certifications the program may require.

What I do is sit on your side of the table while the work itself gets defined. That means helping you build a realistic restoration budget, reviewing scope for constructability, separating urgent stabilization from longer-term restoration, and making sure the work your contractor prices actually lines up with what the program is likely to credit. A surprising amount of credit value gets lost simply because work was scoped or documented in a way that did not fit the qualifying categories. Getting that right early is the same discipline that helps you [avoid change orders]({% post_url 2025-12-03-how-to-avoid-change-orders %}) later.

## A realistic way to think about it

Do not let the credit drive the project. Let the building drive the project, and let the credit be a reason to do the work properly rather than cheaply. The houses and timber-frame barns in this valley have survived a long time by being repaired by people who understood them. The tax programs, at their best, reward exactly that.
